Exploring Space Biology Through AI: My Experience with MCP-GeneLab

Exploring NASA's biological datasets, knowledge graphs, and what AI-assisted tools could mean for scientific research.

October 2026 · By Kavya Krishnamurthy · 7 min read

I've been looking for opportunities to get more involved in computational biology research, particularly projects where I can apply my background in computer science and bioinformatics while learning from real scientific work.

While independent learning and personal projects have been valuable, I've also wanted to contribute to something beyond my own experiments. Even in a small capacity, being involved in the development of research tools felt like a good place to start.

That's what interested me about volunteering to beta-test MCP-GeneLab, a tool that allows researchers to explore NASA's space biology datasets using AI assistants like ChatGPT.

I was curious about two things: how well an AI assistant could work with structured scientific data, and whether this approach could make biological research more accessible without losing the technical detail that makes the analysis meaningful.

01 / The knowledge graph

Getting familiar with the data

MCP-GeneLab connects to the SPOKE-GeneLab Knowledge Graph, which brings together information from NASA's Open Science Data Repository (OSDR). Essentially, it connects spaceflight missions, studies, experiments, genes, and other biological measurements in a way that allows researchers to explore relationships between them. I started by understanding the structure of the knowledge graph, the organisms represented, and how studies were connected to their assays.

"Describe the SPOKE-GeneLab knowledge graph and visualize its schema."

Coming from a computer science background, I found the knowledge graph itself quite interesting. It's one thing to learn about graph databases, and another to see how they're being used to organize actual biological research. The idea of asking scientific questions in plain English instead of manually navigating datasets was appealing, although I was equally interested in seeing how well it worked beyond simple queries.

02 / Exploring the studies

From genes to microbes

Once I understood the structure, I explored several NASA datasets, including OSD-244, OSD-48, OSD-267, and OSD-161.

OSD-244 OSD-48 OSD-267 OSD-161

With OSD-244, I looked at differential gene expression across experimental comparisons, generated volcano plots, and explored genes shared between different time points.

"Find the common differentially expressed genes and create a Venn diagram to visualize their overlaps."

I then moved to OSD-48, where I investigated DNA methylation alongside gene expression. One of the questions I explored was whether genes with increased promoter methylation also showed reduced expression. This was particularly interesting because it involved connecting two different types of molecular data rather than analyzing them separately.

With OSD-267, I explored microbial abundance data, comparing results from DESeq2 and ANCOM-BC and looking for overlapping bacteria and fungi across assays.

I also worked with OSD-161, exploring experimental factors, differential expression, and human ortholog mapping, along with broader questions about spaceflight studies involving different anatomical structures.

There was quite a bit of switching between biological topics, but that's also what I enjoyed about it. Each dataset introduced a different question, and I could follow that question into another analysis.

03 / Literature connections

The part I found most useful

One thing I hadn't explored much before was connecting different research tools through an AI assistant. During the testing, I used PubMed alongside MCP-GeneLab to search for publications related to genes and microorganisms identified in the analyses.This was probably my favorite part of the experience. Being able to move from exploring a dataset to looking up related scientific literature within the same workflow was genuinely useful.

Of course, finding a paper doesn't mean a biological relationship has been established. There's still the work of reading, evaluating, and interpreting the evidence. But it made the initial exploration much easier. It also gave me a better idea of how tools like MCP could fit into a larger research workflow, particularly when working with different databases and data types.

04 / Beta-testing notes

A few thoughts on the experience

My role was to test the tool, explore its capabilities, document the interactions, and provide feedback. I appreciated how easily I could move between studies, request visualizations, and ask follow-up questions without having to start over each time.

There were also areas where I felt the experience could be improved, particularly clearer explanations of some statistical outputs, easier access to underlying results, and better options for exporting data and figures. I think those details matter when a tool is intended for scientific research. Making data easier to access is useful, but being able to verify and interpret it is just as important.

05 / Personal reflection

Why this experience mattered to me

I'm interested in pursuing research in computational biology, particularly where genomics, computational methods, and AI overlap. At this stage, I'm trying to build that interest into something more practical through independent projects, learning new methods, and finding opportunities to participate in research-related work.

Volunteering for MCP-GeneLab was one such opportunity. It gave me a chance to work with real biological datasets, explore a research tool under development, and contribute feedback from a user's perspective. I also came away with a better understanding of how knowledge graphs and AI-assisted tools could support scientific investigation. And a few more things I'd like to learn, which seems to be the usual outcome whenever I explore something new.

I'm hoping to become more involved in research projects and collaborations as I continue developing my skills. Experiences like this are useful not just for what I learn technically, but also for understanding how research tools are built, evaluated, and used by the scientific community.
